You set up a portrait on your camera, got the light right and took the shot. Before posting, you opened an editor to remove a piece of litter on the pavement behind your subject. Thirty seconds of work.

You uploaded it to Instagram, and the post came back with AI info attached.

You didn't make this picture with Midjourney or ChatGPT. You took it. Now a client is asking whether your work is "real".

This is one of the most common complaints photographers have had about Instagram since 2024. The reason is almost always the same: the editing tool you used wrote a note inside the file saying AI was involved, and Instagram read it. Below are the tools that do this, what they write, and how to check and clean a file before you post it.

The Short Answer

The Hidden "Receipt" Inside Your Photo

Every image file has two parts:

  1. The picture: the pixels you see.
  2. The metadata: text stored inside the file that you don't see.

Think of metadata as a receipt stapled to the back of a print. Your camera writes the first lines: camera model, lens, shutter speed, the time and often your GPS location. That part is called EXIF.

Editing apps add to the receipt. When you use a generative AI feature, many of them now add a line that says, in effect, "AI changed this image". They do it in one of two standard formats:

  • C2PA Content Credentials: a signed record of how the file was made and edited. It's tamper-evident, so it can't be quietly edited, but it can be removed. More on C2PA.
  • IPTC fields: plainer labels such as DigitalSourceType, where the value compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia means "a real photo with AI-made parts" and trainedAlgorithmicMedia means "made by AI".

Instagram doesn't look at your photo the way a person would. It reads the receipt.

Five Editing Tools That Can Trigger the "AI Info" Label

Most people assume only fully generated images get labeled. In practice, everyday retouching tools on desktop and phone can write the same tags. Here's what each one does, based on the companies' own announcements.

1. Adobe Photoshop (Generative Fill, Generative Expand, Generative Remove)

Adobe co-founded the Content Credentials effort, and Photoshop attaches a C2PA manifest when you use its Firefly-powered generative features. The manifest records that generative AI contributed to the image. Expanding a canvas or removing a stranger from the background is enough.

Photoshop's ordinary tools, like the Spot Healing Brush or Content-Aware Fill, aren't generative and aren't the issue.

2. Adobe Lightroom (Generative Remove)

Lightroom's Generative Remove uses Adobe Firefly to erase objects. Adobe said when it launched that Content Credentials would be attached automatically to photos edited with it. The plain Remove tool, without the generative option ticked, doesn't need Firefly.

If you only used AI Denoise or masking, you're less likely to be flagged. Those are AI-assisted but not generative. Still, check the exported file, because export settings and Lightroom versions vary.

3. Samsung Galaxy AI (Generative Edit, Sketch to Image, Portrait Studio)

Since the Galaxy S24, the Gallery app's Generative Edit lets you move or erase objects and fill the gap. Samsung marks these images in two ways:

  • a visible "AI-generated content" watermark in the bottom-left corner of the picture;
  • from the Galaxy S25 and One UI 7, C2PA Content Credentials in the file for images made or changed with Generative Edit, Sketch to Image, Portrait Studio and similar tools.

The visible watermark is part of the picture, not the metadata, so a metadata tool can't remove it. Crop it out first if you want it gone.

4. Google Magic Editor, Magic Eraser and Zoom Enhance

Since October 2024, Google Photos shows "Edited with Google AI" for photos changed with Magic Editor, Magic Eraser or Zoom Enhance. That note comes from IPTC metadata written into the file. Some of Google's generative features, like Reimagine on Pixel phones, also add SynthID, an invisible watermark inside the pixels.

5. Apple iPhone Clean Up (iOS 18.1 and later)

Apple's Clean Up tool in the Photos app removes background objects. Apple marks these edits in the photo's metadata with a note like "Modified with Clean Up".

Apple's note is its own label, not a C2PA manifest, so it's less likely to set off Meta's detection than the tools above. If a Clean Up photo did get flagged, scan it. Reports differ on exactly which fields it writes, so check what your own file contains.

Why Instagram Labels Photos That Are 99% Real

In February 2024, Meta announced that Facebook, Instagram and Threads would label AI images by detecting industry-standard markers: C2PA and IPTC metadata, the same tags described above.

What it reads is whether a tag says AI was involved, not how much of the image AI touched. A photo where AI changed a few hundred pixels carries the same kind of tag as one AI made from scratch. To the system, both files say "AI".

Meta has adjusted this more than once. After photographers complained, it renamed "Made with AI" to "AI info" in July 2024. In September 2024 it moved the label into the post's menu for images that were only edited with AI, and kept it under the username for images it detects as AI-generated. So depending on what your file says, the label may sit under your name or behind the three-dot menu.

What Metadata Cleaning Can and Can't Fix

It can

  • remove C2PA Content Credentials from Photoshop, Lightroom and Galaxy AI edits;
  • remove IPTC, XMP and EXIF fields that mention AI editing;
  • remove GPS location and camera serial numbers at the same time;
  • do all of that without changing a single pixel.

It can't

  • Remove watermarks in the picture itself. Google's SynthID is woven into pixel values, and Samsung's "AI-generated content" mark is drawn onto the image. Neither is metadata.
  • Beat visual AI detection. Platforms can also run classifiers that look at the image, not the file. Meta has said it's working on this, and cleaning metadata has no effect on it.
  • Change posts that are already live. A cleaned file only helps the next upload. To fix an existing post, you'd delete it and upload the cleaned copy as a new one.

When You Should Keep the Label

Removing a misleading label from a real photo is fair: the photo is real, and one AI-assisted fix doesn't change that. Hiding that a picture was made by AI is different.

  • Real photo, small AI fix (a stranger, a sign, dust, a stray wire): cleaning the metadata gives a more accurate picture of what you posted.
  • Image generated from a prompt in Midjourney, ChatGPT or Firefly: keep the disclosure. Meta requires people to label realistic AI-generated video and audio and lets you add the label to images yourself. Your audience will trust you more for it.
  • Heavy AI changes (new sky, swapped faces, added people): if a viewer would feel misled without knowing, leave the label on.
